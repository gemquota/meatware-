# Rights and Licensing Decision Brief

**Status:** Decision required. Planning brief only; not legal advice or a contributor agreement.

## Why decide now
The project is publicly documented and may eventually become a commercial film franchise. A permissive code licence, documentation licence and film/story rights arrangement solve different problems. Applying one generic licence to everything without deciding the production model could create serious conflicts.

## Separate asset classes

| Asset class | Examples | Decision required |
|---|---|---|
| Code and tooling | Scripts, schemas, automation | Open-source or restricted licence; dependencies and notices |
| Documentation | README, process guides, technical docs | Reuse, adaptation and redistribution terms |
| Story IP | Premise, characters, outlines, scripts, dialogue | Copyright ownership, adaptation and production permissions |
| Visual/audio assets | Artwork, generated video, music, sound, voice | Source rights, model/tool terms, performer consent, usage scope |
| Contributor submissions | Issues, PRs, suggestions | Whether accepted material may be used; treatment of rejected work |

## Models to evaluate

### A. Public-domain dedication / CC0-style model
Maximum reuse freedom. May be unsuitable if the project wants to control a coherent commercial franchise or preserve attribution expectations.

### B. Permissive open licence
Allows broad reuse, including commercial reuse, subject to terms. Could enable others to use franchise material independently.

### C. Share-alike Creative Commons model
Requires downstream adaptations to use compatible terms. May conflict with conventional film financing, distribution and rights clearance.

### D. Source-available public repository with explicit contribution permission
The public can inspect and discuss material, but reuse and commercial production remain controlled by copyright and a separate permission or contributor agreement. This may preserve franchise control but creates administration and permission requirements.

### E. Split licensing by asset class
Code can use an open-source licence while story IP, scripts, artwork and production assets have separate terms. More precise, but requires an asset inventory and consistent notices.

## Questions for the maintainer
1. Is Meatware open source, source-available, or a public collaboration repository?
2. Who owns pre-existing franchise material?
3. Does a contributor retain copyright in original accepted work?
4. What permission does the project need to edit, adapt, combine, distribute and commercially produce accepted work?
5. Does permission cover sequels, remakes, marketing, merchandise, games and derivative media?
6. Is permission exclusive or non-exclusive?
7. What consideration, if any, is offered? If none, say so clearly.
8. How are rejected submissions treated?
9. How are AI-generated materials, model terms, training restrictions and human authorship represented?
10. What happens if a contributor withdraws permission or disputes attribution?
11. Are voice, likeness, performance, music and third-party assets subject to separate releases?
12. Which jurisdiction and dispute process apply?

## Interim recruitment boundary
Until these questions are resolved:
- Solicit critique, factual feedback, continuity findings and non-exclusive suggestions only.
- Do not request finished scripts, artwork, music, voice performances or substantial production assets for incorporation.
- Do not tell contributors that issue comments grant commercial rights.
- Do not promise compensation, ownership, royalties, employment or screen credits.
- Do not solicit confidential or third-party restricted material.

## Approval checklist
Before accepting substantial creative material for production, obtain a reviewed written policy or agreement covering asset class, rights granted, permitted uses, attribution, consideration, AI disclosure, warranties, withdrawal/termination and governing law. Seek qualified legal advice for the final instrument.
